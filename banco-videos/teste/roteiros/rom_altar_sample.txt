Everyone said I hired her for revenge. They were right.

Ten years ago, Clara Mendes left me standing at the altar of Saint Michael's, in front of two hundred guests, holding a ring that had belonged to my grandmother. No note. No call. Her sister just walked down the aisle alone, whispered she's gone, and my whole life folded in half while the organ was still playing.

So yes. When her resume landed on my desk a decade later - she didn't know the company had changed names, didn't know the CEO was me - I signed the hire before I finished my coffee. Not because she was qualified. Because I wanted her close enough to watch me become everything she walked away from. I wanted her to clock in, every morning, into the empire built by the man she humiliated.

Controversial? Maybe. Cruel? Absolutely. I'm not the hero of this story. I just thought I was the victim of it.

For three months, I was surgical. The impossible deadlines. The meetings scheduled at seven in the morning. The reports I made her redo twice just because I could. She never complained. Not once. She would just nod, take her files, and stay after everyone left, the office lamp burning over her desk like a small stubborn lighthouse.

That was the first crack in my plan: she was not sorry the way I wanted her to be sorry. She was sorry in a way I didn't understand.

The second crack was the bracelet. Thin, silver, a little anchor charm. I won it in a claw machine on our third date, back when we were twenty-three and broke and sure of everything. It cost me four dollars. She wore it every single day, under the sleeve of her blazer, where she thought nobody could see.

You don't wear a four-dollar bracelet for ten years out of guilt. You wear it out of grief.

The third crack broke me. It was a Tuesday night, raining like the sky had something to confess. She had gone home; her desk drawer was half open. I'm not proud of what I did - I told you, I'm not the hero - I opened it. Inside there was a pharmacy bag, a bus ticket to her mother's town, and an envelope. Old. Yellowed. My name on it, in her handwriting. Sealed. Dated the morning of our wedding.

I sat in her chair, in my own company, in the dark, holding a letter that had waited ten years for me. And I read it.

My love. This morning Doctor Ferraz called about my results. It is not fatigue. It is my heart - the same thing that took my mother at thirty-one. He gave me two years, maybe three. You have a ring, a future, and the whole world ahead of you. I watched my father spend a decade dying alongside my mother, and it hollowed him into a man neither of us recognized. I will not do that to you. You will hate me, and your hate will keep you safe. Marry someone who gets to grow old. I have loved you since the claw machine. I will love you from wherever I go. Clara.

There was a second page. A doctor's letter, dated four years later. Misdiagnosis. Treatable. Managed with simple medication - the pharmacy bag suddenly made sense. She had been given her life back, two years after she had already burned ours down to protect me.

And she never came to tell me. Because by then I was rich, and briefly engaged, and famous in the business magazines - and she decided she had lost the right.

She kept the letter in her work drawer. Where she could see it every day. Next to a decade of newspaper clippings. All of them about me.

I drove to her apartment in that rain. I didn't call. When she opened the door and saw me holding the envelope, she didn't ask how, or why. She just said: you were supposed to hate me forever.

And I said the only true thing I had left: I tried. I hired you just to ruin your life. Turns out I can't even do that without falling in love with you again.

We talked until four in the morning. About her heart - the one that works fine now, and the one that never stopped being mine. About the wedding guests, and her mother's grave, and the four-dollar anchor still on her wrist. I asked why she never sold it. She said anchors are for people who plan to come back.

We didn't rush. We had a first date again, at a cafe where we were both too nervous to eat. I signed her transfer to another department the next morning - you can't fall for someone who reports to you, and I very much intended to keep falling.

Last June, at Saint Michael's, in front of thirty guests this time, Clara Mendes walked the whole aisle. Slowly. Smiling. On time.

Some people get one love story. We got two - with a ten-year intermission, and a villain in the middle who turned out to be love itself, wearing the mask of goodbye.
